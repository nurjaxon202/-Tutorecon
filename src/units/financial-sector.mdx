## Financial assets

People hold wealth in different forms. Each form trades off three things: **liquidity** (how easily it can be used to buy things), **rate of return**, and **risk**.

- **Money** is the most liquid asset but earns little or nothing.
- **Stocks** are shares of ownership in a company. They can pay dividends and rise in value, but they can also fall.
- **Bonds** are loans to a company or government. The borrower promises to pay interest and repay the amount borrowed on a set date.

### Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions

Say you own a bond that pays $50 a year and cost $1,000, a 5% return. Then interest rates rise and new bonds pay 10%. Nobody will pay $1,000 for your bond when a new $1,000 bond pays $100 a year. To sell it you have to cut the price until its $50 payment is a competitive return. So <mark>when interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall</mark>, and when rates fall, bond prices rise.

## What money is

**Money** is anything widely accepted as payment. It has three functions:

- **Medium of exchange**: you can trade it for goods, so you do not have to find someone who has what you want and wants what you have.
- **Unit of account**: prices are measured in it, so you can compare values.
- **Store of value**: it holds value over time, so you can save.

The money supply is measured in groups. **M1** is the most liquid money: currency in circulation and checkable deposits. **M2** adds less liquid "near-monies" such as savings deposits, small time deposits, and money market funds. In 2020 the US Federal Reserve moved savings deposits into M1 in its official data, so if a question depends on the definition, use the one your course gives you.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Credit cards are not money. Using one creates a loan that you pay back later with money. Your checking account balance is money; your credit limit is not.
</Callout>

## How banks create money

Banks keep only a fraction of deposits in reserve and lend out the rest. This is **fractional reserve banking**. When the borrower spends the loan, the money ends up deposited in another bank, which lends out part of it again. Each round adds new deposits, which is new money.

- **Required reserves** = deposits × the reserve requirement.
- **Excess reserves** = total reserves − required reserves. This is the amount a bank can lend.
- The **money multiplier** = 1 ÷ reserve requirement. It gives the maximum total change in deposits from new excess reserves.

<Worked title="Following a deposit through the banking system" prompt="Someone deposits $1,000 in cash into a bank. The reserve requirement is 10%. How much can this bank lend, and what is the maximum change in the money supply from loans?">
  <Step n={1} title="Required and excess reserves">
    Required reserves are 10% × $1,000 = $100. The other $900 is excess reserves, so the bank can lend up to $900.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="The money multiplier">
    1 ÷ 0.10 = 10.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Maximum change from new loans">
    $900 × 10 = $9,000 of new money created through lending.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="Why not $10,000?">
    The original $1,000 was already money when it was cash. Depositing it only changed its form from currency to a checking balance, so it does not add to the money supply. If the Fed had instead bought a $1,000 bond from the bank, all $1,000 would be new excess reserves, and the maximum change would be $10,000.
  </Step>
</Worked>

The real multiplier is smaller than this maximum, because people hold some cash and banks sometimes keep extra reserves.

## The money market

The **money market** shows how the nominal interest rate is set in the short run.

- **Money demand** slopes down. The nominal interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money instead of an interest-paying bond, so when rates are high people hold less money. Money demand shifts right when the price level or real GDP rises, because people need more money for transactions.
- **Money supply** is vertical. It is set by the central bank and does not depend on the interest rate.

## Monetary policy

The **Federal Reserve** (the Fed) is the central bank of the United States. **Monetary policy** is its use of tools to change the money supply and interest rates.

In a **limited-reserves** system, the tools are:

- **Open market operations**: buying or selling government bonds. When the Fed buys bonds, it pays with new reserves, so the money supply grows. This is the tool AP questions use most.
- **The discount rate**: the interest rate the Fed charges banks for short-term loans.
- **The reserve requirement**: the share of deposits banks must hold.

Today the Fed operates with **ample reserves**. Banks hold so many reserves that small changes in supply do not move rates, so the Fed steers short-term rates mainly by setting the **interest it pays on reserves**. Raising that rate makes banks less willing to lend at low rates, which pushes market rates up.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Goal | Expansionary (fight a recession) | Contractionary (fight inflation) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open market operations | Buy bonds | Sell bonds |
| Discount rate | Lower it | Raise it |
| Reserve requirement | Lower it | Raise it |
| Interest on reserves | Lower it | Raise it |
| Effect on interest rates | Fall | Rise |
| Effect on AD | Shifts right | Shifts left |

</div>

Write monetary policy as a chain, and do not skip links: Fed buys bonds → money supply rises → nominal interest rate falls → investment and interest-sensitive consumption rise → AD shifts right → real GDP and the price level rise.
</Callout>

## The loanable funds market

The **loanable funds market** brings together savers and borrowers, and it sets the **real** interest rate.

- **Supply** comes from saving by households, firms, and governments running surpluses, and from foreign savers.
- **Demand** comes from borrowing: mainly firms that want to invest, and governments that run deficits.

<Graph model="loanable-funds" caption="When the government borrows more, it competes with private borrowers for the same funds." />

When the government runs a deficit and borrows, demand for loanable funds rises and the real interest rate goes up. Higher rates discourage some private investment. That is **crowding out**, and it is one reason economists debate how much deficit spending actually adds to the economy over time.

<Callout kind="mistake">
The money market uses the **nominal** interest rate, and money supply is set by the central bank. The loanable funds market uses the **real** interest rate, and supply comes from saving. Use the market the question asks for, and label the axis correctly.
</Callout>
