An interest rate is only as good as what the money can buy. This topic separates the rate written on a loan from the rate that matters for buying power, and it explains who wins when inflation surprises everyone.

## Nominal and real

- The **nominal interest rate** is the rate stated on a loan or account.
- The **real interest rate** is the nominal rate adjusted for inflation. It measures the change in buying power.

**Real interest rate = nominal interest rate − inflation rate**

## The Fisher equation

When a loan is made, lenders and borrowers only know **expected** inflation. So they set:

**Nominal rate = real rate + expected inflation**

If lenders want a 3% real return and expect 2% inflation, they charge 5%. If expected inflation rises to 5%, the nominal rate rises to 8%. This is why high expected inflation brings high nominal interest rates.

## Expected versus actual

- **Expected real rate** = nominal rate − expected inflation, known when the loan is made.
- **Actual real rate** = nominal rate − actual inflation, known afterward.

When **actual inflation is higher than expected**, the actual real rate is lower than planned: **borrowers gain** and **lenders lose**. When actual inflation is lower than expected, lenders gain.

## Worked example

<Worked title="The surprise" prompt="A bank lends at 6%, expecting 2% inflation. Inflation turns out to be 7%. What real interest rate did the bank expect, and what did it actually earn?">
  <Step n={1} title="Expected">
    6% − 2% = 4%.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Actual">
    6% − 7% = −1%. The bank was repaid in dollars that buy less than the dollars it lent.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Who gained">
    The borrower, who paid a real rate of −1% instead of the expected 4%.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Calculate the real rate from the nominal rate and inflation, or the nominal rate from the real rate and expected inflation.
- Explain who gains and who loses when inflation is higher or lower than expected.
- Remember which graph uses which rate: the money market uses the **nominal** rate, the loanable funds market uses the **real** rate.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Adding inflation to get the real rate. The real rate subtracts inflation from the nominal rate. Adding them gives you nothing meaningful.
</Callout>
