Gross domestic product is the single most important number in macroeconomics. This topic covers what it counts, what it leaves out, and how the circular flow shows that spending, income, and output are three views of the same thing.

## The circular flow

In the simplest model, **households** sell factors of production (labor, land, capital) to **firms** in **factor markets** and receive income. They spend that income buying goods and services from firms in **product markets**. Money flows one way around the circle, and goods, services, and resources flow the other way.

A fuller model adds:

- **Government**, which collects taxes and buys goods and makes transfer payments
- The **financial sector**, which turns saving into lending for investment
- The **foreign sector**, through exports and imports

**Leakages** take money out of the spending stream: saving, taxes, and imports. **Injections** add it: investment, government purchases, and exports.

## What GDP measures

**GDP** is the market value of all **final** goods and services produced **within a country** in a **given year**.

- **Final** means sold to the end user. Intermediate goods, like the flour a bakery buys, are left out to avoid counting them twice.
- **Within the country** means location, not ownership. Cars made by a Japanese company in Ohio count in US GDP.
- **In a given year** means production, not sales. A used car sold this year was counted when it was made.

## The expenditure approach

**GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)**

- **C, consumption:** household spending on goods and services.
- **I, investment:** business spending on capital (machines, factories), new housing, and changes in business inventories. Buying stocks or bonds is **not** investment in GDP.
- **G, government purchases:** spending on goods and services. **Transfer payments** like Social Security are **not** included, because nothing is produced in exchange.
- **X − M, net exports:** exports minus imports.

GDP can also be measured by adding up **income** (wages, rent, interest, and profit) or the **value added** at each stage of production. All three give the same answer, because every dollar spent is a dollar of income to someone.

## On the exam

- Decide whether a transaction is counted in GDP and in which component.
- Calculate GDP from a table using C + I + G + (X − M). Leave out transfers and financial purchases.
- Identify leakages and injections in the circular flow.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Counting stock purchases as investment. In GDP, investment means spending on new physical capital, new housing, and inventories. Buying a financial asset only changes who owns it.
</Callout>
