Monetary policy is how a central bank, the Federal Reserve in the United States, steers interest rates to stabilize prices and employment. The course covers two systems: limited reserves and ample reserves.

## The goals

The Fed’s dual mandate is **stable prices** and **maximum employment**. It fights recessions with **expansionary** (easy) policy and inflation with **contractionary** (tight) policy.

## Tools in a limited-reserves system

When banks hold few excess reserves, the Fed changes the money supply:

- **Open market operations:** buying or selling government bonds. **Buying bonds** adds reserves to banks, so they lend more and the money supply rises. **Selling bonds** does the opposite. This is the most-used tool in this system.
- **The discount rate:** the interest rate the Fed charges banks for loans. Lowering it encourages bank borrowing and lending.
- **The reserve requirement:** lowering it lets banks lend more of each deposit.

## Tools in an ample-reserves system

Today banks hold large reserves, so small changes in reserves do not move rates. The Fed instead sets **administered rates**:

- **Interest on reserve balances (IORB):** the rate the Fed pays banks on their reserves. Banks will not lend to others for less than they can earn at the Fed, so **raising IORB raises** the federal funds rate and other short-term rates, and **lowering it lowers** them.
- The Fed also uses a rate on overnight reverse repurchase agreements to keep short-term rates in its target range.

The **federal funds rate** is the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves. It is the Fed’s main policy target.

## The chain of effects

**Expansionary:** buy bonds (or lower IORB, the discount rate, or the reserve requirement) → lower interest rates → more investment and interest-sensitive consumption → **AD shifts right** → real GDP and the price level rise, unemployment falls.

**Contractionary:** sell bonds (or raise IORB, the discount rate, or the reserve requirement) → higher interest rates → less investment and consumption → **AD shifts left** → real GDP and the price level fall.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Saying the Fed buys bonds to fight inflation. Buying bonds adds money to the economy and lowers rates. To fight inflation, the Fed sells bonds or raises its administered rates.
</Callout>
