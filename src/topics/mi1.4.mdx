Why do countries trade, even when one of them is better at making everything? The answer is comparative advantage, and it is tested every year, often as a full free-response question.

## What you need to know

A producer has an **absolute advantage** in a good if it can make more of that good with the same resources (or the same amount with fewer resources).

A producer has a **comparative advantage** in a good if it can make that good at a **lower opportunity cost** than another producer.

Trade depends on comparative advantage, not absolute advantage. When each producer specializes in the good it makes at the lower opportunity cost and they trade, both can consume combinations **outside** their own production possibilities curves.

A producer cannot have a comparative advantage in both goods. If one producer gives up less of good Y to make X, the other must give up less of X to make Y.

## Output and input problems

AP questions give the numbers in one of two ways. The method is different, so read the table heading first.

**Output problems** tell you how much each producer can make in a fixed time. Use “other over” (OOO): the opportunity cost of one unit of a good is the *other* good’s number divided by this good’s number.

**Input problems** tell you how much time or how many resources it takes to make one unit. Use “IOU” (input, other goes under): the opportunity cost of one unit is this good’s number divided by the other good’s number.

## Terms of trade

The **terms of trade** are the price at which the two goods are exchanged. For both sides to gain, the terms must fall **between** the two producers’ opportunity costs.

If a phone costs Country A 2 shirts to make and costs Country B 3 shirts, any price between 2 and 3 shirts per phone helps both. At 2.5 shirts, A sells phones for more than they cost it, and B buys phones for less than it would cost to make them.

## Worked example

<Worked title="Output problem" prompt="In a day, Country A can make 40 shirts or 20 phones. Country B can make 30 shirts or 10 phones. Who has the comparative advantage in each good, and what terms of trade would help both?">
  <Step n={1} title="Opportunity cost of a phone">
    A: 40 ÷ 20 = 2 shirts per phone. B: 30 ÷ 10 = 3 shirts per phone. A has the lower cost, so A has the comparative advantage in phones.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Opportunity cost of a shirt">
    A: 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 phone per shirt. B: 10 ÷ 30 = about 0.33 phone per shirt. B has the comparative advantage in shirts.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Absolute advantage">
    A makes more of both goods, so A has the absolute advantage in both. That does not change who should specialize.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="Terms of trade">
    Any price between 2 and 3 shirts per phone helps both, for example 1 phone for 2.5 shirts.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Identify absolute and comparative advantage from an output or input table.
- Calculate opportunity cost per unit and say which producer should specialize in which good.
- Pick terms of trade that benefit both, or explain why a proposed price would not.
- FRQs often ask you to draw each country’s PPC from the numbers first. Straight lines, with the intercepts from the table.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Mixing up output and input tables. If the table says “hours to make one unit,” a bigger number means a producer is worse at the good. Using the output formula on input data flips every answer.
</Callout>
