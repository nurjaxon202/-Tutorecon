Deficits and debt are easy to mix up, and questions about them usually test whether you know which is a yearly flow and which is a running total.

## Deficit and debt

- A **budget deficit** happens in a **single year** when government spending is greater than tax revenue.
- A **budget surplus** happens when revenue is greater than spending.
- The **national debt** is the **total** amount the government owes: the sum of all past deficits minus all past surpluses.

A deficit adds to the debt. A surplus can pay some of it down. The government covers a deficit by **borrowing**, which means selling bonds.

## How the economy changes the deficit

Even with no new laws, the deficit moves with the business cycle because of **automatic stabilizers** (Topic 3.9):

- In a **recession**, tax revenue falls and transfer payments rise, so the deficit grows.
- In an **expansion**, the deficit shrinks.

Expansionary fiscal policy (more spending, lower taxes) makes the deficit larger. Contractionary fiscal policy makes it smaller.

## Costs of a large debt

- **Interest payments** take up more of the budget, leaving less for other programs.
- Heavy borrowing can raise interest rates and **crowd out** private investment (Topic 5.5), slowing growth.
- Debt held by foreigners means future interest payments flow abroad.
- Future generations may face higher taxes or lower spending to pay for it.

Economists disagree about how much debt is too much, and borrowing during a deep recession is widely seen as reasonable.

## On the exam

- Tell a deficit (one year) from the debt (total).
- Calculate the change in debt from a deficit or surplus.
- Predict how a recession or a policy changes the deficit.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Saying the debt falls when the deficit gets smaller. A smaller deficit still adds to the debt, just less. The debt falls only when there is a surplus.
</Callout>
