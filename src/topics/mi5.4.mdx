A monopsony is a market with a single buyer of a factor, like the only large employer in a remote town. It is the mirror image of a monopoly, and it explains why a minimum wage can raise employment in some markets.

## Why MFC is above the wage

A monopsonist faces the whole upward-sloping **market labor supply curve**. To hire one more worker, it must raise the wage, and it pays the higher wage to **every** worker, not just the new one. So the cost of one more worker (MFC) is **greater than the wage**. The **MFC curve lies above the labor supply curve**.

## The monopsony outcome

1. Hire where **MRP = MFC**.
2. Pay the **lowest wage that attracts that many workers**, read from the **labor supply curve**.

Compared with a competitive labor market, a monopsonist:

- hires **fewer workers**,
- pays a **lower wage**,
- pays workers **less than their MRP**,
- creates **deadweight loss**.

## Minimum wage in a monopsony

In a competitive labor market, a minimum wage above equilibrium lowers employment. In a monopsony, a minimum wage set **between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage** can **raise both the wage and employment**. The minimum wage makes MFC flat at that wage up to the supply curve, so the firm no longer has to raise everyone’s pay to hire one more worker.

## Worked example

<Worked title="MFC from a table" prompt="A monopsonist must pay $10 to hire 1 worker, $11 each to hire 2, and $12 each to hire 3. What is the marginal factor cost of the third worker?">
  <Step n={1} title="Total cost of labor">
    1 worker: $10. 2 workers: $22. 3 workers: $36.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="MFC of the third worker">
    $36 − $22 = $14, which is more than the $12 wage, because the other two workers also get a raise.
  </Step>
</Worked>
