Price discrimination means charging different buyers different prices for the same good when the difference is not explained by cost. Student discounts, airline fares, and coupons all use it.

## Conditions

A firm can price discriminate only if:

1. It has **market power** (a downward-sloping demand curve).
2. It can **separate buyers into groups** with different willingness to pay or different elasticities, for example with a student ID or by when they book.
3. It can **prevent resale** between groups. If students could resell cheap tickets to adults, the scheme would collapse.

The group with **more elastic demand** gets the **lower price**. Vacation travelers are price sensitive and book early, so they pay less than business travelers who must fly on short notice.

## Perfect price discrimination

**Perfect (first-degree) price discrimination** means charging each buyer exactly their maximum willingness to pay. It changes the monopoly outcome in three ways:

- The MR curve becomes the **demand curve itself**, because selling one more unit does not lower the price on earlier units.
- The firm produces where **demand meets MC**, the **allocatively efficient quantity**. There is **no deadweight loss**.
- **Consumer surplus is zero.** All of the surplus goes to the firm as profit.

So perfect price discrimination is efficient but moves all the gains from trade to the seller.

## Worked example

<Worked title="Five buyers, one seller" prompt="Five buyers will each buy one unit and will pay at most $10, $8, $6, $4, and $2. Marginal cost is $5, with no fixed cost. Compare a single price with perfect price discrimination.">
  <Step n={1} title="Single price">
    At $10 the firm sells 1 unit for $5 profit. At $8, 2 units, profit (8 − 5) × 2 = $6. At $6, 3 units, profit $3. The best single price is $8, with $6 profit.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Perfect price discrimination">
    Sell to everyone who values the good above $5, charging each their value: $10 + $8 + $6 − $15 = $9 profit.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Compare">
    Output rises from 2 to 3 units, the efficient amount. Profit rises from $6 to $9, and consumer surplus falls to zero.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Name the conditions needed to price discriminate.
- Identify which group pays more, using elasticity.
- Compare perfect price discrimination with a single-price monopoly: output, efficiency, consumer surplus, and deadweight loss.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Calling different prices for different costs “price discrimination.” Charging more to ship a package farther reflects a higher cost. Price discrimination is different prices for the same good with the same cost.
</Callout>
