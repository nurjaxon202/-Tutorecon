Every economics question starts from one fact: people want more than the world can make. This topic sets up the vocabulary the rest of both courses uses, so a clear picture here saves points later.

## What you need to know

**Scarcity** means our wants are unlimited, but the resources used to satisfy them are limited. Because of scarcity, every person, firm, and government has to make choices, and every choice gives something up.

Scarcity is not the same as a shortage. A shortage is temporary and happens when a price is below equilibrium. Scarcity never goes away. Even a billionaire faces it, because time is limited and choosing one activity means giving up another.

Economists sort the resources used to make things into four **factors of production**:

<div class="table-scroll">

| Factor | What it includes | What its owner earns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land | All natural resources: soil, oil, water, forests, minerals | Rent |
| Labor | Human effort, physical and mental | Wages |
| Capital | Tools, machines, and buildings made to produce other goods | Interest |
| Entrepreneurship | Organizing the other three and taking the risk of a business | Profit |

</div>

Two words trip students up. **Physical capital** is equipment, like a delivery truck or a pizza oven. **Human capital** is the skill and knowledge in workers, like a nurse’s training. Money is not capital in economics. Money buys resources, but it produces nothing by itself.

## Microeconomics and macroeconomics

**Microeconomics** studies individual decision makers: a household choosing what to buy, a firm choosing how many workers to hire, a single market for coffee. **Macroeconomics** studies the economy as a whole: total output, the unemployment rate, inflation, and economic growth.

A question about why the price of eggs rose is micro. A question about why prices across the whole economy rose is macro.

## Positive and normative statements

A **positive statement** describes the world and can be tested with evidence, even if it turns out to be false. A **normative statement** says what should be, so it depends on values.

- Positive: “A higher minimum wage reduces the number of teenage jobs.”
- Normative: “The minimum wage should be raised to $20 an hour.”

Words like *should*, *ought*, *fair*, and *better* usually signal a normative statement. A positive statement can be wrong and still be positive, because what makes it positive is that data could settle it.

## On the exam

- Multiple-choice questions ask you to classify an example as land, labor, capital, or entrepreneurship. Watch for money and for human capital.
- You may be asked to pick the positive statement from four normative ones, or the reverse.
- Scarcity is the reason behind opportunity cost and the production possibilities curve, which come next. Expect it as the “why” in those questions.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Calling money “capital.” In everyday speech people raise capital for a business, but on the exam capital means tools, machines, and buildings. A bank account balance is not a factor of production.
</Callout>
