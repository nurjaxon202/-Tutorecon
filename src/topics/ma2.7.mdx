Real GDP does not grow smoothly. It rises and falls around a long-run trend, and those swings are the business cycle. The AD-AS model in Unit 3 explains why they happen.

## Phases of the cycle

- **Expansion (recovery):** real GDP rises, unemployment falls, and inflation may pick up.
- **Peak:** the high point, where real GDP stops rising.
- **Contraction (recession):** real GDP falls and unemployment rises. A common rule of thumb calls two quarters of falling real GDP a recession.
- **Trough:** the low point, where the contraction ends and a recovery begins.

The trend line through the cycle is **potential output**, the real GDP the economy makes at full employment. Over time it rises with long-run growth.

## Output gaps

- When real GDP is **below potential**, there is a **negative output gap** (a **recessionary gap**). Unemployment is above the natural rate because of cyclical unemployment.
- When real GDP is **above potential**, there is a **positive output gap** (an **inflationary gap**). Unemployment is below the natural rate, and prices tend to rise faster.
- At **potential output**, cyclical unemployment is zero, and unemployment equals the natural rate.

## How the indicators move together

<div class="table-scroll">

| | Real GDP | Unemployment | Inflation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Expansion | Rising | Falling | Often rising |
| Contraction | Falling | Rising | Often falling |

Unemployment usually keeps rising for a while after a recession ends, because firms wait to see whether the recovery lasts before they hire.

## On the exam

- Label the phases of a business cycle graph.
- Connect each phase to changes in unemployment and inflation.
- Identify a recessionary or inflationary gap from real GDP and potential GDP.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Saying unemployment is zero at the peak or at full employment. Even at potential output, frictional and structural unemployment remain. Only cyclical unemployment is zero.
</Callout>
