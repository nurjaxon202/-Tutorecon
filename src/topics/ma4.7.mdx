The loanable funds market links saving and borrowing, and it sets the real interest rate. It explains why big government deficits can raise interest rates and crowd out private investment.

## The two curves

- **Supply of loanable funds** comes from **saving**: households, businesses, government surpluses, and foreign savers. It slopes up, because a higher real interest rate rewards saving.
- **Demand for loanable funds** comes from **borrowing**: businesses that want to invest, and governments that run deficits. It slopes down, because a lower real rate makes more investment projects worth doing.

The vertical axis is the **real interest rate**, and the horizontal axis is the **quantity of loanable funds**.

## What shifts each curve

**Supply shifts right** when:

- households save more (for example, because of a tax break for saving),
- the government runs a surplus or a smaller deficit (in the approach where public saving is on the supply side),
- foreign savers put more money into the country.

**Demand shifts right** when:

- businesses expect higher profits from new capital, or get an investment tax credit,
- the government borrows more to cover a deficit.

## Crowding out

When the government borrows more, demand for loanable funds rises (or supply falls, depending on how the graph is drawn). Either way, the **real interest rate rises**, and **private investment falls**. That is **crowding out** (Topic 5.5). Less investment today can mean less capital and slower growth later.

## Money market versus loanable funds

<div class="table-scroll">

| | Money market | Loanable funds market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interest rate | Nominal | Real |
| Supply | Vertical, set by the central bank | Saving, slopes up |
| Demand | Money demand | Borrowing for investment and deficits |
| Used for | Monetary policy | Fiscal policy, crowding out, capital flows |

</div>

## Worked example

<Worked title="A budget surplus" prompt="The government moves from a balanced budget to a surplus. What happens in the loanable funds market?">
  <Step n={1} title="Which curve?">
    The government adds to national saving (or borrows less), so the supply of loanable funds shifts right.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Result">
    The real interest rate falls, and the quantity of funds borrowed for private investment rises.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Explain crowding out and its long-run effect on growth.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Mixing up the two interest rate graphs. A change in the money supply belongs in the money market (nominal rate). Government borrowing belongs in the loanable funds market (real rate).
</Callout>
