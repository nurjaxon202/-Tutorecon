Why do some countries have 2% inflation and others 200%? In the long run, the answer is almost always how fast the money supply grows. The quantity theory of money makes that link precise.

## The equation of exchange

**M × V = P × Y**

- **M:** the money supply
- **V:** the **velocity of money**, how many times a dollar is spent on final goods in a year
- **P:** the price level
- **Y:** real output (real GDP)

P × Y is nominal GDP, so V = nominal GDP ÷ M.

## The quantity theory of money

Classical economists assume that in the long run:

- **velocity is stable**, because payment habits change slowly, and
- **real output is set by resources and technology**, not by money.

Then any increase in M must show up in P. In growth rates:

**% change in M + % change in V ≈ % change in P + % change in Y**

If velocity is constant and real output grows 2% a year, money growth of 6% a year leads to inflation of about 4%.

## Money is neutral in the long run

The idea that money affects only prices, not real output, in the long run is called the **neutrality of money**. It matches the vertical LRAS and LRPC. In the short run, money can affect output, which is why monetary policy is used to fight recessions.

## Hyperinflation

When a government pays its bills by printing money, the money supply can grow extremely fast, and prices follow. That is the usual cause of **hyperinflation**.

## Worked example

<Worked title="Use the equation" prompt="The money supply is $2 trillion and velocity is 5. What is nominal GDP? If real output is $5 trillion, what is the price level?">
  <Step n={1} title="Nominal GDP">
    M × V = $2 trillion × 5 = $10 trillion.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Price level">
    P = MV ÷ Y = $10 trillion ÷ $5 trillion = 2.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Use MV = PY to find nominal GDP, velocity, or the price level.
- Predict inflation from money growth, velocity, and real growth.
- Explain why money growth affects prices, not real output, in the long run.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Saying more money makes a country richer in the long run. With stable velocity and output set by real resources, more money just means higher prices.
</Callout>
