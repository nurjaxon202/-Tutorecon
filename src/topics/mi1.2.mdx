Every society has to answer three questions: **what** to produce, **how** to produce it, and **for whom**. An economic system is simply the way a society answers them.

## What you need to know

There are three pure systems, and every real country mixes them.

<div class="table-scroll">

| System | Who answers the three questions | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional | Custom and habit | People make what their parents made, in the same way. Common in small farming and hunting communities. |
| Command | A central planner, usually the government | Planners decide output, set prices, and assign jobs. The government owns most resources. |
| Market | Buyers and sellers through prices | Private owners trade freely. Prices send signals about what to make and reward those who make it well. |

</div>

A **mixed economy** relies mainly on markets but gives the government a role: providing public goods like roads and defense, setting rules, protecting property, and redistributing some income. The United States, Canada, and almost every other country today are mixed economies.

## How prices allocate resources

In a market economy, prices do three jobs at once.

- **Signal.** A rising price tells producers that buyers want more of something.
- **Incentive.** The higher price means more profit per unit, so firms want to make more and new firms want to enter.
- **Ration.** The higher price also tells buyers to use less, so the limited supply goes to those who value it most.

When the price of lithium rose sharply as demand for electric car batteries grew, mining companies opened new mines (incentive) and some battery makers looked for other materials (rationing). No planner told them to do either.

## Property rights and the role of government

Markets only work when people can own things and keep what they earn. **Property rights**, enforced by courts, give people a reason to invest, build, and trade. Economists also point to the rule of law, stable money, and enforceable contracts as conditions markets need.

Market systems are praised for efficiency and innovation, and criticized because they can leave some people with very little and can fail when there are externalities or public goods. Command systems can direct resources toward a goal quickly but tend to produce shortages, surpluses, and weak incentives to work or innovate. Unit 6 comes back to when markets fail.

## On the exam

- You may be asked which system answers “what to produce” mainly through prices (market) or through a central plan (command).
- Expect a question about why most economies are mixed.
- Price signals come back in every supply and demand question: a higher price moves resources toward a good.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Treating “market economy” as “no government at all.” Even market economies need a government to protect property rights and enforce contracts. The difference is who makes production decisions.
</Callout>
