Some goods are hard to sell in a market because people can use them without paying, or because one person’s use does not take anything from anyone else. Two questions sort every good into one of four types.

## Rival and excludable

- A good is **rival** if one person’s use leaves less for others. A sandwich is rival. A radio broadcast is not.
- A good is **excludable** if people who do not pay can be kept from using it. A movie ticket is excludable. A fireworks show over a city is not.

<div class="table-scroll">

| | Excludable | Not excludable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rival** | **Private good:** food, clothing, a phone | **Common resource:** ocean fish, clean air, public grazing land |
| **Not rival** | **Club good:** streaming service, toll road with no traffic, cable TV | **Public good:** national defense, a lighthouse, street lights |

</div>

## The free-rider problem

**Public goods** are neither rival nor excludable. Since people can enjoy them without paying, each person has an incentive to let others pay, a **free rider**. A private firm cannot collect enough revenue, so the market **underproduces** public goods or does not provide them at all. That is why governments usually provide them and pay with taxes.

The **marginal social benefit** of a public good is the sum of every person’s marginal benefit at each quantity, because everyone uses the same unit at once.

## The tragedy of the commons

**Common resources** are rival but not excludable. Each user takes as much as they want, because no one can stop them and they do not bear the full cost of the resource running out. The result is **overuse**: overfishing, overgrazing, traffic on free roads. Fixes include quotas, fees, and assigning property rights.

## Worked example

<Worked title="Classify the goods" prompt="Decide whether each good is private, public, a common resource, or a club good: (a) a sandwich; (b) national defense; (c) fish in the open ocean; (d) a toll road that is rarely crowded.">
  <Step n={1} title="(a) A sandwich">
    Rival (once eaten, it is gone) and excludable (the store can refuse to sell): a private good.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="(b) National defense">
    Nonrival (protecting one more person costs nothing extra) and nonexcludable: a public good.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="(c) Ocean fish">
    Rival (a fish caught is gone) but hard to exclude people from: a common resource, at risk of overuse.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="(d) An uncrowded toll road">
    Excludable (the toll) but nonrival while uncrowded: a club good.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Classify a good using rivalry and excludability.
- Explain the free-rider problem and why public goods are underprovided by markets.
- Explain the tragedy of the commons and a policy that reduces overuse.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Calling anything the government provides a public good. Public schools and the postal service are provided by governments, but they are rival and excludable. A public good is defined by its features, not by who pays for it.
</Callout>
