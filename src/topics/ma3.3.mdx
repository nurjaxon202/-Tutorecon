Short-run aggregate supply describes what firms across the economy produce at each price level while some costs, especially wages, are slow to change. Its shifts cause supply shocks like the oil crises of the 1970s.

## What the curve shows

The **short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)** curve shows the quantity of real GDP firms produce at each price level in the short run. It **slopes upward**.

## Why SRAS slopes up

In the short run, many input prices are **sticky**: wages are set by contracts, and some other costs are fixed for a while. When the overall price level rises, the prices firms receive go up faster than their costs, so producing more becomes profitable. When the price level falls, the opposite happens.

## What shifts SRAS

Anything that changes production costs across the economy shifts SRAS:

- **Input prices,** especially **nominal wages** and **energy**. Higher wages or oil prices shift SRAS **left**. Lower ones shift it right.
- **Productivity and technology.** Better technology lowers costs and shifts SRAS right.
- **Expectations of inflation.** If workers expect higher prices, they bargain for higher wages, which shifts SRAS left.
- **Business taxes, subsidies, and regulations.** Higher per-unit taxes or costly regulations shift SRAS left. Subsidies shift it right.
- **Supply shocks:** natural disasters, pandemics, and wars that disrupt production.

A leftward shift of SRAS is a **negative supply shock**: the price level rises while real GDP falls. That combination is called **stagflation**.

## Worked example

<Worked title="An oil price shock" prompt="The price of oil doubles. Show what happens in the AD-AS model in the short run, and describe the effects on the price level, real GDP, and unemployment.">
  <Step n={1} title="Pick the curve">
    Oil is an input for almost every firm, so production costs rise across the economy. That shifts SRAS, not AD.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Pick the direction">
    Higher costs mean firms supply less at every price level, so SRAS shifts left.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Find the new equilibrium">
    Along the unchanged AD curve, the new intersection has a higher price level and a lower real GDP.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="Name the result">
    Lower output means fewer jobs, so unemployment rises while prices rise. That combination is stagflation.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Explain why SRAS slopes up (sticky wages and input prices).
- Name what shifts SRAS and the direction.
- Show a supply shock and describe stagflation.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Shifting SRAS for a change in the price level. A higher price level moves along SRAS. Only a change in costs, productivity, or expectations shifts it.
</Callout>
