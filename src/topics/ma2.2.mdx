GDP measures production well, but it was never meant to measure how well people live. This topic lists what GDP leaves out and why real GDP per capita is a better, though still imperfect, guide to living standards.

## What GDP leaves out

- **Nonmarket production:** cooking at home, caring for your own children, volunteer work. It creates value but has no market price.
- **The underground economy:** cash jobs that are never reported and illegal activity. A large underground economy makes measured GDP **understate** true production.
- **Leisure:** a country where people work fewer hours for the same pay is better off, but GDP does not show it.
- **Quality changes and new products:** a phone that does far more for the same price barely changes GDP.
- **Environmental damage:** a factory’s output adds to GDP, but the pollution it causes is not subtracted.
- **Distribution:** GDP says nothing about how income is divided.

Some spending raises GDP without making people better off than before, like rebuilding homes after a hurricane or paying for more security because crime rose.

## Real GDP per capita

To compare living standards, economists use **real GDP per capita**: real GDP divided by population. “Real” removes price changes (Topic 2.6), and “per capita” adjusts for population size. A country with a large GDP and a huge population can have a lower standard of living than a small country with a smaller GDP.

## Worked example

<Worked title="Compare two countries" prompt="Country A has real GDP of $2 trillion and 100 million people. Country B has real GDP of $1.5 trillion and 50 million people. Which has the higher real GDP per capita?">
  <Step n={1} title="Country A">
    $2 trillion ÷ 100 million = $20,000 per person.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Country B">
    $1.5 trillion ÷ 50 million = $30,000 per person.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Answer">
    Country B, even though its total GDP is smaller.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Name something GDP leaves out and explain why.
- Say whether a change would make GDP overstate or understate well-being.
- Use real GDP per capita, not total GDP, to compare living standards.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Treating a rise in GDP as proof that people are better off. GDP can rise with more pollution, longer working hours, or rebuilding after a disaster.
</Callout>
