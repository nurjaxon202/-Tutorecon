Short-run policy is about closing gaps. Long-run growth is about raising the ceiling: how much the economy can produce at all. Small differences in growth rates add up to huge differences in living standards over a few decades.

## Measuring growth

**Economic growth** is a lasting increase in **real GDP per capita**. On graphs it shows up as:

- an **outward shift of the PPC**, and
- a **rightward shift of LRAS**.

A move from inside the PPC to a point on it is a recovery, not growth.

## Where growth comes from

Growth in real GDP per capita comes mainly from higher **labor productivity**, output per worker or per hour. Productivity rises with:

- **Physical capital:** more and better machines, factories, and infrastructure per worker.
- **Human capital:** education, training, and health.
- **Technology:** new ideas and better ways to combine resources.
- **Natural resources**, though countries with few resources can still grow fast.

More workers raise total real GDP, but they raise **real GDP per capita** only if output per person rises.

## The rule of 70

A quick way to see how growth compounds:

**Years to double ≈ 70 ÷ annual growth rate (in percent)**

At 2% a year, real GDP per capita doubles in about 35 years. At 7%, in about 10.

## Growth per person

**Growth in real GDP per capita ≈ real GDP growth − population growth.** If real GDP grows 3% and the population grows 3%, output per person stays about the same.

## On the exam

- Show growth with an outward PPC shift or a rightward LRAS shift.
- Name sources of productivity growth.
- Use the rule of 70.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Calling a move from a recession back to full employment “economic growth.” That is a return to the PPC. Growth means the PPC itself moves out.
</Callout>
