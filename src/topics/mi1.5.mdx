Economists assume people and firms respond to incentives: they compare what an action costs with what it brings in. Doing that well means counting the right costs, including the ones that never show up as a bill.

## What you need to know

**Opportunity cost** is the value of the next best alternative given up when you make a choice. It is only the single next best option, not the sum of everything you could have done.

Costs come in two kinds:

- **Explicit costs** are direct payments of money: rent, wages, materials, gas.
- **Implicit costs** are the value of resources you already own and use instead of renting them out: the salary you gave up to run your own business, or the interest your savings could have earned.

Economists count both. That is why **economic profit** (revenue minus explicit and implicit costs) is smaller than **accounting profit** (revenue minus explicit costs only). Unit 3 uses this again.

## Rational decisions and incentives

A **rational** decision maker takes an action when its total benefit is greater than its total cost, including opportunity cost. People respond to **incentives**: a tax on sugary drinks makes them less attractive, and a bonus for early work makes staying late more attractive.

**Sunk costs** are costs already paid that cannot be recovered. They are the same whatever you choose next, so they should not affect the decision. A nonrefundable concert ticket is sunk: if something better comes up, the ticket price should not keep you from choosing it.

## Utility

**Utility** is the satisfaction a person gets from consuming a good. Economists assume consumers try to get the most total utility they can from a limited budget. Topic 1.6 shows how they do that one choice at a time.

  <Step n={1} title="Explicit costs caused by the choice">
    Tuition and books, $20,000, are paid only because Jordan goes to college.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Implicit cost">
    The $30,000 job is the next best use of Jordan’s time, so it counts.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Leave out what does not change">
    Room and board are paid either way, so they are not part of the cost of choosing college.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="Add up">
    $20,000 + $30,000 = $50,000.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Calculate an opportunity cost from a short story, leaving out sunk costs and costs that are paid either way.
- Tell explicit from implicit costs.
- Explain a decision with “the benefit is greater than the cost” reasoning.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Adding up every alternative. If you can work for $80 or go to a concert worth $60 and you choose to study, the opportunity cost is $80, the single best thing you gave up, not $140.
</Callout>
