Crowding out is the catch in deficit-financed fiscal policy: government borrowing can push up interest rates and push out private investment. It connects Unit 3, Unit 4, and long-run growth.

## How it works

1. The government runs a deficit and borrows to cover it.
2. In the **loanable funds market**, more government borrowing raises **demand** for loanable funds (or lowers the supply of national saving).
3. The **real interest rate rises**.
4. Higher rates make some business investment projects unprofitable, so **private investment falls**, along with interest-sensitive consumption like home and car purchases.

The expansionary effect on AD is therefore smaller than the simple multiplier predicts.

## Long-run consequences

Investment builds the **capital stock**. If crowding out lowers investment year after year, the economy ends up with less capital, which means slower growth in productivity and **less rightward movement of LRAS** than it would otherwise have had.

## When crowding out is small or large

- **Small** when the economy is in a deep recession with lots of unused saving, when private investment barely responds to interest rates, or when the central bank keeps rates from rising (accommodating monetary policy).
- **Large** when the economy is near full employment, when investment is very sensitive to interest rates, or when the government borrows heavily.

## The open-economy twist

Higher real interest rates attract **foreign capital**, which raises demand for the currency and makes it **appreciate**. Exports become more expensive and imports cheaper, so **net exports fall**, another way the fiscal expansion is partly offset (Unit 6).
