Aggregate demand is the total spending on an economy’s final goods and services. Its shifts drive booms and recessions, and it is the curve that fiscal and monetary policy try to move.

## What the curve shows

The **aggregate demand (AD)** curve shows the quantity of real GDP demanded at each **price level**. It is made of the same four parts as GDP: **AD = C + I + G + (X − M)**.

## Why AD slopes down

AD does not slope down for the same reasons as a single demand curve. A lower price level raises the quantity of real output demanded because of three effects:

- **Real wealth (real balances) effect:** when the price level falls, the money and savings people hold buy more, so they spend more.
- **Interest rate effect:** a lower price level means people need less money for purchases, so they lend out more of it, lowering interest rates. Lower rates raise investment and interest-sensitive consumption.
- **Exchange rate (foreign trade) effect:** a lower price level makes domestic goods cheaper than foreign goods, so exports rise and imports fall.

A change in the price level moves the economy **along** the AD curve.

## What shifts AD

Anything that changes spending at every price level shifts AD:

- **Consumption:** consumer confidence, household wealth (stock and home prices), taxes, expectations about the future.
- **Investment:** business confidence, interest rates (from something other than the price level), expected profits, investment tax credits, new technology.
- **Government purchases:** more government spending shifts AD right.
- **Net exports:** income growth abroad (raises exports), a weaker domestic currency (raises exports and lowers imports), trade policy.

Monetary policy shifts AD by changing interest rates, and fiscal policy shifts it through government spending and taxes.
