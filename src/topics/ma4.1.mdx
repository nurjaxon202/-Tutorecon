Unit 4 is about money, banks, and interest rates. It starts with the assets people use to store wealth, and the trade-off between earning a return and being able to spend.

## Types of financial assets

- **Money:** cash and checking account balances. Completely liquid, but earns little or no interest.
- **Bonds:** loans to a government or company. The buyer (lender) receives interest payments and gets the face value back at maturity. **Bondholders are lenders.**
- **Stocks:** shares of ownership in a company. Stockholders can earn dividends and gains when the price rises, but have no promised return. **Stockholders are owners.**
- **Loans and savings accounts** are also financial assets.

## Liquidity, rate of return, and risk

- **Liquidity** is how easily an asset can be turned into money without losing value. Cash is the most liquid. A house is not very liquid.
- **Rate of return** is what the asset earns, usually as a percentage.
- **Risk** is how uncertain that return is.

There is a trade-off: assets that are more liquid or less risky usually pay **less**. To get people to hold riskier or less liquid assets, they must offer a **higher expected return**.

## Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions

A bond pays a fixed amount of interest. If **market interest rates rise**, new bonds pay more, so an existing bond with a lower fixed payment becomes less attractive. Its **price falls** until its effective return matches the new rate. If market rates fall, existing bonds become more valuable and their prices rise.

This inverse relationship is central to how monetary policy works through open market operations.

## The opportunity cost of holding money

Holding money means giving up the interest you could earn on bonds or other assets. So the **nominal interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money**. When rates rise, people hold less money. That is why the money demand curve slopes down (Topic 4.5).

## Worked example

<Worked title="Bond prices and interest rates" prompt="A bond will pay $1,050 one year from now and nothing else. What is it worth today if the market interest rate is 5%? What if the rate rises to 10%?">
  <Step n={1} title="The pricing rule">
    A buyer pays today the amount that, invested at the market rate, would grow to $1,050. Price = $1,050 ÷ (1 + interest rate).
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="At 5%">
    $1,050 ÷ 1.05 = $1,000.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="At 10%">
    $1,050 ÷ 1.10 ≈ $954.55.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="The lesson">
    The bond’s payment is fixed, so when market interest rates rise, its price must fall to give new buyers the higher return. Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Tell stocks from bonds, and owners from lenders.
- Rank assets by liquidity.
- Explain why bond prices fall when interest rates rise.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Saying bond prices rise when interest rates rise. They move in opposite directions. Higher market rates make older, lower-paying bonds worth less.
