Unit 6 comes full circle here: a change in the exchange rate changes net exports, which is part of aggregate demand, so currency markets can move real GDP and the price level.

## From exchange rate to net exports

- A **depreciation** makes a country’s exports cheaper abroad and its imports more expensive at home. **Exports rise, imports fall, net exports rise.**
- An **appreciation** does the opposite: **net exports fall.**

## From net exports to AD

Net exports are part of **AD = C + I + G + (X − M)**. So:

- **Depreciation → higher net exports → AD shifts right** → real GDP and the price level rise in the short run.
- **Appreciation → lower net exports → AD shifts left** → real GDP and the price level fall.

## Who wins and who loses

When a country’s currency **appreciates**:

- **Exporters and their workers lose**, because their goods cost more abroad.
- **Firms that compete with imports lose**, because foreign goods are cheaper at home.
- **Consumers of imported goods and tourists going abroad gain.**

When it **depreciates**, exporters and import-competing firms gain, while consumers of imports and travelers abroad lose.

## Trade balance

A depreciation tends to move a country’s current account toward surplus (or a smaller deficit), because exports rise and imports fall. An appreciation tends to move it toward deficit.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Treating a strong currency as good for everyone. An appreciation helps buyers of imports and travelers abroad, but it hurts exporters and domestic firms that compete with imports.
