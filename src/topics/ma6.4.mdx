Anything that changes how much people want to buy a country’s goods or assets moves its currency. This topic lists the main causes and how policy works through them.

## What changes the demand for or supply of a currency

- **Relative interest rates.** Higher **real interest rates** in the US attract foreign savers, who demand dollars to buy US assets, so the **dollar appreciates**. Lower US rates do the opposite.
- **Relative price levels (inflation).** Higher inflation in the US makes US goods less attractive, so foreigners demand fewer dollars and Americans supply more dollars to buy imports. The **dollar depreciates**.
- **Relative income.** If US incomes rise, Americans buy more imports, supplying more dollars, so the **dollar depreciates**. If incomes abroad rise, foreigners buy more US exports, so the dollar appreciates.
- **Tastes.** If foreigners develop a taste for US products, demand for dollars rises and the dollar appreciates.
- **Speculation.** If traders expect a currency to rise, they buy it now, which raises its value.

## Policy through the exchange rate

- **Contractionary monetary policy** raises interest rates → foreign capital flows in → **the currency appreciates** → net exports fall.
- **Expansionary monetary policy** lowers interest rates → capital flows out → **the currency depreciates** → net exports rise.
- **Expansionary fiscal policy** financed by borrowing raises real interest rates → the currency **appreciates** → net exports fall.

## Central bank intervention

A central bank can influence its own currency directly. To **stop it from appreciating**, it can **sell** its own currency and buy foreign currency, adding to supply. To **support** a weak currency, it can buy its own currency with foreign reserves.
