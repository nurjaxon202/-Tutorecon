Supply describes sellers. It mirrors demand, with one important twist: the things that shift supply are mostly about the cost of producing.

## What you need to know

**Supply** is the set of quantities sellers are willing and able to sell at each possible price. The **law of supply** says that, other things equal, a higher price raises the **quantity supplied**. The supply curve slopes up because a higher price makes it worth producing units that cost more to make, and because the marginal cost of producing usually rises with output.

As with demand, a change in the good’s own price is a **movement along** the curve. Everything else that affects sellers **shifts** it.

## What shifts supply

- **Input prices.** Higher wages, energy, or material costs shift supply **left**. Cheaper inputs shift it **right**.
- **Technology.** Better methods lower costs and shift supply right.
- **Taxes and subsidies.** A per-unit tax on producers shifts supply left (up by the amount of the tax). A per-unit subsidy shifts it right.
- **Prices of other goods the seller could make.** If corn becomes more profitable, farmers plant less wheat, so wheat supply shifts left.
- **Expectations.** If sellers expect a higher price later, they may hold back supply now, shifting today’s supply left.
- **Number of sellers.** More firms in the market means more supply.
- **Natural events.** A drought or frost cuts farm supply. A good harvest raises it.

An increase in supply shifts the curve **right**, which can be read two ways: sellers offer more at every price, or they are willing to sell each quantity at a lower price.

## Individual and market supply

**Market supply** is the sum of every seller’s supply at each price, added sideways. If three identical firms each offer 100 units at $5, market quantity supplied at $5 is 300.

## Worked example

<Worked title="Shift a supply schedule" prompt="At prices of $2, $4, and $6, sellers offer 100, 200, and 300 units, so quantity supplied is 50 times the price. Wages rise, and sellers now need $1 more per unit to offer each quantity. What do they offer at $4 now?">
  <Step n={1} title="Read the old rule">
    Before, quantity supplied = 50 × price. At $4, that was 200 units.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Apply the cost increase">
    Each quantity now needs a price $1 higher: 100 units at $3, 200 at $5, and 300 at $7. The new rule is quantity = 50 × (price − 1).
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Answer">
    At $4, sellers now offer 50 × 3 = 150 units, 50 fewer than before.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="Name the change">
    Less is offered at every price, so supply shifted left (up by $1). The rise in wages is a supply shifter, not a movement along the curve.
  </Step>
</Worked>

## On the exam

- Name the direction of a supply shift from a story about costs, technology, taxes, or the number of firms.
- Know that a tax on sellers shifts supply, not demand, and a subsidy shifts supply right.
- Combine with demand in Topic 2.7 to find what happens to price and quantity.

<Callout kind="mistake">
Saying a higher price shifts supply right. A higher price moves sellers up along the curve to a larger quantity supplied. The curve itself shifts only when something other than the good’s own price changes.
</Callout>
